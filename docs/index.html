What is space weather?

The Sun is constantly boiling off a stream of charged particles called the solar wind. Most days it blows past Earth harmlessly at around 400 km/s, deflected by our magnetic field.

Sometimes the Sun does something more violent. A coronal mass ejection is a billion tonnes of magnetised plasma flung outward at up to 3,000 km/s. When one hits Earth, it compresses and shakes our magnetic field. That shaking induces electric currents in anything long and conductive on the ground (power lines, pipelines, railway signalling), and it heats and inflates the upper atmosphere, dragging on satellites.

The standard measure of the resulting disturbance is the Kp index, a 0-9 scale updated every three hours. Kp < 3 is a quiet day. Kp 5 and above is an official geomagnetic storm.

Why anyone cares

Three real events, with measured costs.

< 1 min
Quebec grid collapse, 13 March 1989
A geomagnetic storm destabilised the Hydro-Québec transmission grid at 2:44 a.m. The whole province went dark for more than nine hours.
$500M
US farm losses, May 2024
The Gannon storm pushed GPS positions off by up to 230 feet. Around 70% of US planted acreage relies on GPS auto-steer, and it hit during peak planting.
6×
Air density at 400 km, May 2024
Thermospheric density rose to six times its pre-storm value. One satellite's orbital decay went from 38 to 180 metres per day; thousands manoeuvred at once.

What I built

An end-to-end pipeline that ingests hourly solar-wind measurements from NASA OMNI2 and real-time data from NOAA SWPC, flags anomalous behaviour, and scores those flags against the five largest geomagnetic storms since 2000.

  • Two detectors. A 3σ statistical process-control baseline, and an Isolation Forest, using the same features and the same scoring so the comparison is fair.
  • An evaluation module that reports event-wise and point-wise metrics side by side, plus a quiet-period false-alarm rate.
  • A scheduled job that scores live NOAA data every morning and publishes a status report.
  • An interactive dashboard where you can move the detection threshold and watch precision and recall trade off in real time.

The metric I refused to report

Storms are rare, roughly 5% of hours in my data. A model that flags nothing at all is therefore 95% accurate. Accuracy is worse than useless on rare-event problems: it actively rewards doing nothing. It appears nowhere in this project, on purpose.

The finding: the simple model won

Every configuration detected every storm, so catching them was not the hard part. The hard part is restraint: not crying wolf the rest of the time. On that, the statistical baseline beat Isolation Forest on every measure.

Watch out for the recall trap

Isolation Forest fitted on quiet data has the highest recall of anything I tested, at 0.661. It gets there by flagging 1,172 hours, roughly a third of every month. If I had reported recall alone, the worst model would have looked like the winner. That is exactly what the quiet-period false-alarm rate is there to catch.

Why the simple model won

The revealing case is October 2003, the Halloween storms. It is the hardest month in the dataset for every detector, but they do not fail equally. The baseline degrades gracefully to an F1 of 0.343. Isolation Forest collapses to 0.062, which is essentially noise.

The reason is the interesting part. That month had elevated geomagnetic activity in 54% of its hours. It was mostly disturbed. Isolation Forest has no external idea of what "normal" means; it infers normal from whatever data you give it. Feed it a month that is mostly storm, and storm becomes normal, so it loses the ability to call it out. The 3σ baseline is anchored to an explicit quiet reference, so its notion of normal survives intact.

The part that generalises

Unsupervised anomaly detection defines "anomalous" as rare in the training data. That definition quietly fails whenever the base rate shifts, which is precisely when a monitoring system matters most. If your fraud model, your equipment-failure model, or your intrusion detector learns "normal" from a period that turns out not to be normal, it will go quiet exactly when you need it loudest.

What this is not

This is a demonstration of method, not an operational forecasting tool. NOAA SWPC does the real job with physics-based models, dedicated spacecraft, and human forecasters. A precision of 0.394 means most alerts are still wrong, so nobody should run infrastructure on it.

Known limitations, stated rather than buried:

  • Five events is a small validation set. Every configuration catches all five, so event recall cannot separate them.
  • The ground truth under-labels reality. The catalogue lists one storm per month-long window, but October 2003 had storm-level Kp in 17% of its hours. Precision figures are lower bounds.
  • No lead-time results yet. Storm onset times are not yet in the catalogue, so I can measure detection but not how much warning it would give.
